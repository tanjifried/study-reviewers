Easy explanation

System architecture is the high-level organization of a system: its parts, connections, and design rules. It shapes how the system behaves and how well it meets needs.

Example: A home security system combines cameras, sensors, and remote access into one coordinated solution.

Key terms to remember
  • System architecture: The foundational organization of components, their relationships, and principles guiding system design and evolution.
Slide 4 of 18 · Open in PDF
Easy explanation

Components do jobs, connectors let components communicate, and configurations describe how they are arranged. These choices define system structure and behavior.

Example: A web app may have a user interface component that calls a service through an API and stores data in a database.

Key terms to remember
  • Components: Functional architectural units such as a database, microservice, or user interface.
  • Connectors: Communication pathways between components, such as API calls or message queues.
  • Configurations: The arrangement of architectural components and connectors.
Slide 5 of 18 · Open in PDF
Easy explanation

A monolith packages the application as one closely connected codebase and deployment unit. It can be straightforward for a small system, but growing complexity can make changes and scaling harder.

Example: A small shop site might deploy its catalog, checkout, and account features together as one application.

Key terms to remember
  • Monolithic architecture: A single unified codebase and deployment unit, often simpler initially but harder to scale and maintain as complexity grows.
Slide 6 of 18 · Open in PDF
Easy explanation

Microservices split an application into smaller independent services. Teams can develop, deploy, or scale services separately, but must handle the extra coordination between them.

Example: A shop could run catalog and payments as separate services, each updated on its own schedule.

Key terms to remember
  • Microservices architecture: Small, independent, loosely coupled services that support separate development, deployment, and scaling.
Slide 7 of 18 · Open in PDF
Easy explanation

A layered design separates responsibilities into levels, such as presentation, business logic, and data access. Clear boundaries make the system easier to understand and change.

Example: A screen asks the business layer to place an order; that layer uses the data layer to save it.

Key terms to remember
  • Layered architecture: A separation into horizontal layers such as presentation, business logic, and data access.
Slide 8 of 18 · Open in PDF
Easy explanation

An architectural style is a broad way to organize a system; a pattern is a reusable solution to a more specific design problem. Patterns can be used within a style.

Example: Layered is a style; MVC is a pattern that separates model, view, and controller responsibilities.

Key terms to remember
  • Architectural style: A high-level structural organization with a system vocabulary and constraints, such as client-server or peer-to-peer.
  • Architectural pattern: A reusable solution to a recurring architectural design problem, such as MVC or Broker.
Slide 9 of 18 · Open in PDF
Easy explanation

Performance can be viewed through response time (how long a response takes), throughput (how much work is handled over time), and latency (the delay before data arrives). Architecture affects all three.

Example: A service may respond in 200 ms, handle 500 requests per second, and still have network delay between services.

Key terms to remember
  • Response time: The time taken for a system to react, such as a website responding to a request.
  • Throughput: The amount of data or number of transactions processed per unit of time.
  • Latency: Delay in data transfer, such as network lag.
Slide 10 of 18 · Open in PDF
Easy explanation

Security includes checking who a user is (authentication), what they may do (authorization), preventing improper data changes (integrity), and keeping sensitive data private (confidentiality).

Example: A bank app verifies a customer’s identity, permits access only to their account, and protects transaction data from changes or exposure.

Key terms to remember
  • Authentication: Verifying a user's identity.
  • Authorization: Granting appropriate access rights to a user.
  • Data integrity: Protection against unauthorized modification of data.
  • Confidentiality: Protecting sensitive information from unauthorized disclosure, for example through encryption.
Slide 11 of 18 · Open in PDF
Easy explanation

Scalability is handling more demand, by adding machines (horizontal) or upgrading a machine (vertical). Maintainability is how easily the system can be changed, repaired, or updated.

Example: Add another server to handle more visitors; use modular code and documentation to make a later feature easier to add.

Key terms to remember
  • Scalability: The capacity to handle increasing workload or demand without unacceptable performance degradation.
  • Horizontal scaling: Adding more servers or instances to handle increased demand.
  • Vertical scaling: Upgrading the hardware resources of an existing server.
  • Maintainability: The ease of modifying, updating, or repairing a system, supported by modularity, clear code, and documentation.
Slide 12 of 18 · Open in PDF
Easy explanation

Reliability describes how consistently a system works without failing over time. Availability describes how often it is up and accessible; quick recovery can keep availability high even when failures occur.

Example: A service that fails occasionally but restarts in seconds may be highly available, though less reliable than one that rarely fails.

Key terms to remember
  • Reliability: The probability that a system performs its intended function without failure over a specified period.
  • Availability: The proportion of time a system is operational and accessible to users.

Easy explanation

Architecture involves trade-offs: improving one quality can make another harder to achieve. Designers should understand which goals matter most and what each choice costs.

Example: Encryption can improve confidentiality while adding processing time that affects performance.

Key terms to remember
  • Architectural trade-off: A compromise where improving one quality attribute can reduce another, such as encryption overhead affecting performance.
Slide 14 of 18 · Open in PDF
Easy explanation

A monolith is one unit, often simpler to deploy and keep transaction data together. Microservices allow independent changes and scaling, but add coordination, monitoring, and data-consistency challenges.

Example: A small product may favor a monolith; separate services may help when parts need to scale or release independently.

Key terms to remember
  • Microservices operational complexity: Independent services introduce challenges including service discovery, distributed transactions, monitoring, and data consistency.
Slide 15 of 18 · Open in PDF
Easy explanation

ATAM is a structured way to evaluate an architecture using scenarios and quality goals. Stakeholders use it to surface risks and trade-offs early.

Example: Ask how the design would handle a sudden tenfold increase in requests, then examine performance risks.

Key terms to remember
  • ATAM: The Architecture Trade-off Analysis Method: a structured, scenario-based evaluation of quality goals, risks, and trade-offs with stakeholder participation.
Slide 16 of 18 · Open in PDF
Easy explanation

An Architectural Decision Record (ADR) captures an important choice, its context, options, and reasons. It gives future teams a record of why the system was designed that way.

Example: An ADR can explain why the team selected a relational database over a NoSQL option.

Key terms to remember
  • Architectural Decision Record / ADR: A historical record of an important architectural choice, capturing context, options, and rationale.
Slide 17 of 18 · Open in PDF
Easy explanation

Architecture changes as needs, technology, and business priorities change. Re-evaluation helps keep the system useful and can reveal technical debt before it grows.

Example: A service that once fit a small user base may need a new design as usage expands.

Key terms to remember
  • Architectural evolution: Continual adaptation of architecture to changing requirements, technology, and business strategy.

Easy explanation

The conclusion highlights that architecture shapes system behavior, that styles bring different trade-offs, and that quality goals and evaluation help guide sound decisions.

Example: Before choosing a style, identify the system’s most important goals and consider how to evaluate them.
